> April 23rd, 2024

> Dear Diary,
> 
> Today was a remarkable Tuesday with an afternoon more enjoyable than most! When the bell rang after classes, I remembered the Intwararumuri Unity and Reconciliation Club meeting. Since it's the 19th day after the President's commencement of commemoration for the Genocide Against the Tutsi, I thought of a relevant theme: Promoting Social Cohesion In Youth. I dream of collaborating with the Rose Castle Foundation as I strive to become an agent of reconciliation within and beyond the Princeton community.

> I'd say money given to the elders in the Productive Safety Net Programme (PSNP)—a government program to support community elders financially—is not enough to cover their basic needs. When I interned as the voice of elders at the Buruhukiro PSNP, I grew my argumentative skills to convince my supervisors about specific issues to pilot a shared change in my community. 
> 
> It all started when an old woman arrived at our offices, claiming she ate once a day and could go days without eating. My co-workers and I were confused and could not address her concern. My supervisor, Aime, asked, "Do they not receive sufficient funding from PSNP to meet their necessities?" "No way! They get more than enough," Peter said. After Peter's statement, I could not help but speak up.
> 
> I emphasized that the life of the elderly was not as easy as the people in the office assumed. Old men usually had difficulty having drinks, although they claimed they'd rather drink than eat. These old men and women receive a monthly income of 50,000 Ethiopian Birr, their only revenue source. "Do you think that money can afford monthly meals in this country of increased market prices? Let alone the drinks," I insisted. From that point on, my colleagues began to understand that our old parents were enduring harsh times: hunger spells, limited hygiene, and mental stress from failing to afford their necessities.
> 
> A few days later, we hosted a meeting with the PSNP beneficiaries in my community to hear from them all. Once given time, one elder stressed what I had mentioned in our office conversation, and many others nodded in agreement. I recorded all these issues in my journal. After hearing from almost all attendees, the host promised we would answer their call later and dismissed the meeting. We headed back to our offices to discuss how we could effectively care for our grand people.
> 
> In front of the committee, I read all the concerns I had recorded. Peter suggested we talk to the PSNPheadquarters and pitch for nationwide solutions. However, knowing the bureaucracies of the pursuit, I suggested that we cut a portion of bonuses and fringe income to uplift struggling community elders as they were in dire need, waiting for a long-term response. Upon acceptance of my proposal, I realized that everyone needs the truth about the subject matter to make an impact. In addition, I found a reason to live so that I can utilize my persuasive power and join those who are determined to change the world by using the truth behind the problems facing communities, much like mine, where embezzlement sometimes robs people of their prospective benefits.
> 
> At Princeton, I will be a student with a fair share of truthful anecdotes that will deepen the Tigers' commitment to challenging unreal treatments for those around them. In addition, I'll tour the Princeton Ridge with Tigers, cracking debatable jokes that will leave lifetime memories and connections, strengthening the Princeton alumni network.
