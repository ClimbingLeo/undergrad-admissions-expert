At UChicago, where the "why" and "how" are asked in symphony in pursuit of the truth, I've found the perfect program to take things apart and put them back together again, making them stronger and better than ever before.

Mia pranced through the rolling green hills that meet the deep blue waters of the coast, her nose enchanted by every new smell she encountered. To her, it seemed as though the landscape kept secrets; there was something surprisingly mysterious about the land that surrounded her. As days went by, and she delved deeper into the island's wonders, she realized that something was missing. 
> 	In her wandering, Mia noticed that travelers were generally accompanied by others, and those accompanied by others exuded a feeling of safety and contentment. She longed for this feeling, and she yearned for a place to call home. However, unlike humans, she was not born into a home; rather, a home would have to find her.

As she kept walking down the shore, her eyes caught a glimpse of a female with long blonde hair rising from the sea. It took her a couple of minutes to recognize her; it was La Pincoya, the water spirit that roamed the waters of Chiloé. In amazement, Mia watched as La Pincoya took gentle care of the marine life and danced, extending out her arms towards the sea. Although full of adrenaline and astonishment for what she just witnessed, Mia felt like something was still missing. The marine life and La Pincoya could call the waters that surrounded Chiloé home, but Mia could not. She continued her journey to the inner island, where she encountered many chilotes who, full of kindness, fed her and provided water. Everyone was thoughtful, but she still had no place to call home.

She felt as though she was receiving a comforting hug in the middle of the night, surrounded by familiarity and warmth. And she was not the only one who felt it. The little girl spent the following days with Mia, taking her everywhere they visited, touring the island together. After 5 days filled with that feeling of safety and contentment that once seemed unreachable, she watched as the girl, crying, packed all her belongings. Mia didn't quite understand what was happening and could only see her begging her parents for something they didn't allow. The following day the girl was gone. Mia was, once again, void of that feeling that she had loved so much, which had made those five days feel like an eternity. 	
> As years passed, Mia became older; she became tired. She had lived a good life, met kind chilotes and had many adventures. However, she longed for that girl who filled her with joy. Desperate to rekindle this feeling, she decided to embark on one last adventure towards la Cueva de Quicaví where, according to the legend, resided the Brujos de Chiloé (the Warlocks of Chiloé). Although now a stuffed animal, Mia retained the same familiar look in her eyes, full of the same hope and spirit that had once made the little girl so happy. 
> Promising to never let her go again, Mia now lives in the bed of the once little girl and reminisces of the crazy adventures that she led during her dog life. While they were not able to verbally communicate, they didn't have to. The feeling of warmth, of belonging, of home went beyond anything either of them could say. As she laid on the bed next to the girl, Mia realized that, all along, she wasn't looking for a place to call home. Home wasn't a place, it was a person. Home was her.

> Prompt: Cats have nine lives, Pac-Man has three lives, and radioactive isotopes have half-lives. How many lives does something else—conceptual or actual—have, and why?
> 
> Commander Nick Reyes could've been immortal. While wading through a sludge of sparking machine parts and enemy gore, the many avoidable deaths of the Call of Duty: Infinite Warfare protagonist could be dealt with just a callous shrug or a sigh, before picking up my DualShock 4 controller again and restarting from my latest checkpoint. Except my Commander Reyes wasn't immortal. He had only a single life. A too-slow crouch, a poorly aimed shot—a single mistake would mark an irreversible end to his story.
> 
> From Gilgamesh and Emperor Qin to Peter Thiel and Jeff Bezos, humankind has been perpetually enraptured by the quest for infinite life. Whether pursuing the Philosopher's Stone or the Holy Grail, immortal elixirs of all kinds have enraptured yet eluded us. Then why, when immortality was a simple click away, did I choose for my video game avatar to be under the jurisdiction of the Reaper? Upon my flickering monitor on that afternoon long ago were six options. Recruit… Regular… Veteran… At the end of the list, was a button in a highlight of deep scarlet—#YOLO. You Only Live Once. Inexplicably, and only with a moment's hesitation, I clicked. 
> 
> What did I gain from tormenting myself unnecessarily in my videogame—choosing to play whilst effectively tightroping above the pits of virtual hell rather than securely seated upon my beanbag, numb and nonchalant due to the prospect of my avatar's certain resurrection? 
> 
> Cats may have nine lives, Pac-Man may have three; we humans have only one. Perhaps my impulse to introduce human fragility into the existence of Commander Nick Reyes wasn't a masochistic taste for challenge, but rather a manifestation of my desire to feel genuinely connected with him—to make his stakes more personal, his mission more urgent, and his choices more important. By giving Reyes mortality, I gave him meaning.
> 
> First-person-shooter games are often criticized for gameplay centered around mindless mowing of dehumanized enemy hordes. Raw adrenaline frequently substitutes for missing emotion and regard for the fallen. Yet perhaps the most corrosive aspect of FPS games is overlooked—the fundamental lack of concern for protagonists' lives that conventional game design fosters.
> 
> In his poem De rerum natura, Roman philosopher Lucretius argued that death is not a fundamental evil. Rather, the canonical end of life provides life with urgency and value. It imbues moments with viscerality—a minute with a sweetheart behind school bleachers, a child born, a momentous career victory—these instances gain meaning through their finite nature. The brevity and unpredictability of life makes every choice, from the life-changing to the mundane, meaningful. An immortal Commander Nick Reyes wouldn't understand that reality. My Nick Reyes does.

### 04 ❌ 真实未录取
**申请专业：** 未指定  
**申请年份：** 2024/2025  
**学生数据：** SAT 1480

> The seven liberal arts in antiquity consisted of the Quadrivium — astronomy, mathematics, geometry, and music — and the Trivium — rhetoric, grammar, and logic. Describe your own take on the Quadrivium or the Trivium. What do you think is essential for everyone to know?
> 
> The Quadrivium and the Trivium, together as a heptagon of subjects, can be regarded as a formidable yet lackluster attempt at standardizing what is the mere basis for an individual to be considered well-rounded. Apart from the obvious geometrical complications of stating the roundedness of a heptagon, the monolithic nature of the curriculum discredits an essential component necessary to achieve a complete understanding of human nature and, with it, of its surroundings: the sense of humor.
> First of all, it is necessary to acknowledge the fact that, by necessity, any set pathway to acquire knowledge has to leave behind branches as important as the ones chosen: the Quadrivium, for example, neglects the study of theater by embracing that of music. Although both could be argued to be equally important, the argument presented by the Pythagoreans in favor of music explains its election as responding to it being "the relations between quantities" and, hence, complimentary to the other three elements that indulge in different studies of quantities.
> As so, it is understood that no addition to the Quadrivium or the Trivium can respond to an aim to increase its scope but it should, rather, increase its optimality. As the base of the liberal arts education, the seven classical arts are not a compendium but a bedrock upon which further studies can be built: therefore, the explanation of each element of its two branches gravitating around singular elements makes sense. In particular, I find that of the Trivium as satisfactory: "Grammar is concerned with the thing as-it-is-symbolized. Logic is concerned with the thing as-it-is-known. Rhetoric is concerned with the thing as-it-is-communicated.".
> Yet, I find it incomplete: its three-way nature not only provokes an arithmetic imbalance with that of the Quadrivium that might result in an asymmetric comparison drawn with it, but it also misses a fundamental component of the "thing". It discusses it to its realized extent, but not to its desired one, and human nature is driven by desires: the sole existence of the seven liberal arts is a proof of that, since it answers to a disquietude in regards to the achievement of integrality.
> And here is why I consider the addition of humor to the Trivium to be not only convenient but also necessary. The study of the seven liberal arts as they are understood nowadays focuses on the tangible state of nature, but it ignores its desired one: it might turn its students into masters of analyzing a book, but can a complete study of its contents turn its students into masters of creating one?
> Humor is all about wishes and desires. The act of laughter is nothing but an ode to the what-if: it responds to an idealistic view of nature that, if properly applied, can turn into significant contributions to Humanity's overall pool of wisdom. Think of a joke, the prime carrier of humor: "something, such as a funny story or trick, that is said or done in order to make people laugh". Its conception arises from a single, well-defined purpose: to create laughter, a physiological expression of amusement. Of idealism, of "the thing as-it-is-wished".
> 
> Similarly, the addition of humor would not only bring an optimistic dimension to the Trivium, but also a sense of enhanced understanding to those who would partake in its study. By learning the art of humor, one develops a new way of connecting concepts that does not limit itself, but can abide by the realms of reality. Think, for example, of the following joke extracted from "Jokes: Philosophical Thoughts on Joking Matters" by Ted Cohen:
> According to Freud, what comes between fear and sex?
> Fünf.
> Even though this joke does not come directly from a study of Freud's body of work nor does it represent any serious conclusion about it, its understanding ties together a basic familiarity with German and his work that, at the same time, allows for a free interpretation of the reader: its mere existence could even be conceived as a ridicule to Freudian reasoning or as a reflection on its German heritage, all based on the perspective through which it is understood. In ten words, two fields that require a certain previous knowledge come along with an exercise in reflection thanks, simply, to the idealistic nature of jokes.
> At last, the addition of humor to the Trivium branch of the liberal arts formation would represent a relaxation to its otherwise rigorous nature. There is no way of teaching a sense of humor or to have a shared cultural basis to it: since humor lies right at the core of the cultural identity of many societies around the world, it is impossible to achieve such a thing as a global general knowledge of humor. I, as a non-American, cannot really grasp Saturday Night Live: it does not respond to my idealized vision of a society and, hence, I do not get it.
> Having resolved the merits for humor to be added to the Trivium as enough, there is an unaddressed question: would adding a new branch to it change its name? Its answer poses, by itself, another reason to add it. It would be funny to have four parts of a set named after the Latin noun for "the place where three roads meet". It can also serve self-assessment purposes, since if the absurdity of it is not understood, a revision of the curriculum could be immediately advised to the student.

> The indigo sky hung above me, shadows and light blending together like the hazy edges of a memory I just couldn't shake. It had to be the color of my regrets—those moments I replayed endlessly, trying to rewrite the unchangeable. I'd grown used to carrying their weight, convinced that every misstep etched an irreversible mark on my future. However, somewhere between yesterday's vanishing echoes and tomorrow's hushed promise, I found myself standing in twilight, caught in a hue that told me something new for the first time in my life: let it go. If I were to give this feeling a name, I'd call it washed in indigo. 
> I had felt this way many times before. For instance, I regretted not choosing Allied over Science and Engineering for my high school program. It seemed like such a pivotal decision, and I couldn't shake the thought that perhaps I had sacrificed my chance to pursue something more aligned with my strengths. Then there were the angered moments on the tennis court, such as losing a doubles game that I should've easily won. The disappointment of every missed shot replayed in my mind for days. Even in extracurriculars, I questioned whether my emphasis on research in computer science was the right decision or if I had let other interests go. 
> This wasn't just about one regret or another—it was about how I let each one define me. The fear of failing again loomed over my decisions as if every choice were a permanent brushstroke I couldn't erase. I let the fog of regret cloud my confidence and impede my growth. But like twilight giving way to dawn, I realized that indigo wasn't just the color of my doubts—it was also the color of possibility. It signified that I could stop replaying old mistakes and start to reinvent how I moved forward. 
> Washed in indigo represents the feeling of release when you stop letting the past define you and you live the present and for the future. Indigo, the color of twilight, symbolizes reflection and transition—standing on the edge of what was while stepping toward what could be. To be washed in indigo is to feel removed from all past mistakes, carrying only the wisdom of past choices while allowing yourself to move forward in life.
> The washed-in also represents a blur—the uncertainty and subjectivity of memory. Just like our view of the past, it isn't always clear—details fade, distort, or soften over time. This blur symbolizes how the past doesn't have to maintain sharp control over us; it can fade into the background as we move forward. Indigo itself is a transitional color, containing both the calmness of blue, which represents reflection, and the transformative depth of violet which symbolizes vision.
> For years, I let the past take control of my present and steer my future. Every mistake felt sharp and permanent, a mark I couldn't erase. But indigo's blurred edges taught me otherwise. It showed me that the past doesn't have to define me—it can guide me, shaping my growth without limiting my future. Losing that tennis match wasn't a definitive failure; it was a lesson in perseverance. Choosing SNE over Allied didn't close doors—it opened new ones I hadn't considered. Now, instead of seeing regret in the places I used to, I see possibilities. Being washed in indigo changed how I look at life, helping me approach it with an open mind and embrace mistakes as part of a bigger journey. I take risks, knowing that even if the outcome isn't perfect, the experience will add value to my life. I've learned to embrace apprehension, trusting that clarity comes with time and reflection.
> I hope washed in indigo inspires the same in others, the freedom to forgive the weight of the past, to accept the blur of imperfection, and to live in the twilight of endless possibility. Life isn't about avoiding mistakes but about letting those moments blend into the masterpieces of who we become. Washed in indigo reminds us to surrender to life's flow and trust that every moment, no matter how uncertain, is a chance to start fresh.

> I've always been drawn to the phrase "caught red-handed," but if I were to create my own, it would be "tickled teal." It seems to represents the feeling of unexpected joy that sneaks up on you when you least expect it. Imagine a moment of surprise that fills you with lightness, excitement, or even an easy smile, just like a gentle tickle but in the form of a soothing teal color that calms yet also uplifts. This expression feels just like a particular moment in my life.
> 
> It was the first summer of quarantine that I. I had gone down a rabbit hole of Vincent van Gogh's lesser-known works. One piece that stood out was "A vase with five sunflowers." The man who painted sunflowers represented gratitude and hope through them despite grappling with profound struggles when he created these masterpieces. They evoke similar emotions within me too. I love sunflowers, and I love his art style, so what could be better than an incorporation of the two? Its duller counterpart, "A vase with twelve sunflowers," being more popular feels like unintentional symbolism—the grander scheme of things at play. The vibrant colours and visible strokes, the symbolism of some flowers being dead and some alive—everything resonates with something deep within me. I wished to create such a masterpiece myself.
> The first step was to sketch out the piece. I took my time, meticulously drawing every line and redoing it until my inner perfectionist was satisfied. Step two: Blocking in the colours. I proceeded with the same level of caution and perfectionism. Everything was progressing flawlessly and I was elated. The next step was my favourite: enhancing the initial blocking and incorporating details. Watching the painting slowly piece together and come to life made me feel like a magician. I began adding the visible strokes, only to have them merge with the blocked colours. No depth was achieved and my canvas looked bulky. Turning to the internet for assistance, I began watching tutorials on the impasto technique. After practising on scrap canvases for a few days, I finally had the confidence to complete my sunflower painting. I carefully orchestrated every stroke—its length, placement, and even colour. Mixing, streaking and then allowing it to partially dry before repeating the ritual. I laboured away at my workstation for days. It was now time to let it dry. It remained covered on my desk for several days. Finally, the oil paints had set and I unveiled them. But what came out was a chunky mush of paint, not remotely similar to Van Gogh's work or my vision.
> The painting sat in a corner of my room, out of my usual line of sight, until my friend paid a visit. She saw it and instantly fell in love. When she playfully mentioned that I had made it for her, I could not bear to disappoint her and claimed I did. She was ecstatic and I had a way to dispose of the canvas without guilt—it was a win-win situation. She called me when hanging it up and showed that she had inscribed one of his quotes underneath: "I dream my paintings and then I paint my dream". Just like the color teal—cool and relaxing, yet vibrant in its own right—it struck me that joy doesn't necessarily come from the results but from the process itself. That moment of being "tickled teal" wasn't about how the painting turned out. It was about finding happiness in the spontaneity of the moment, and it's a feeling I carry with me now, knowing that life often offers its brightest joys when you least expect them.
> The epiphany followed as to what I had done wrong. Micromanaging every stroke is what caused them to blend together. The brush guides the artist, not the other way around. The sentiment in Van Gogh's works cannot be preplanned, despite every stroke existing with purpose. Similar is the way of life.
> The "failure" that now hangs on my friend's wall wasn't without purpose either. As I sit here, reminiscing about my venture into impasto, the parallels between myself and that canvas become clearer. I may be at a low point—much like that paint—but that doesn't render my existence meaningless. I affect people around me the way that painting affected my friend. For the first time, I think about myself. What am I like? I realise that I am kind and empathetic. I smile and help others, hoping to make someone's day. I crave art; I look into things deeper than they may be. The sky, the stars, the flowers and everything around me becomes the subject of my masterpieces. I teach those acquainted with me how I look at the world. The lens that I look at the world with, oil paints helped me look at myself through it. And I found myself. I found hope. 
> My existence may still be insignificant in the grander scheme of things. But if you zoom in—to a more personal level— I know that I have impacted others. I have left my brushstrokes in their lives and will continue doing so.
