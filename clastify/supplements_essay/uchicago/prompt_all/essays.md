At UChicago, where the “why” and “how” are asked in symphony in pursuit of the truth, I’ve found the perfect program to take things apart and put them back together again, making them stronger and better than ever before.

Mia was, once again, void of that feeling that she had loved so much, which had made those five days feel like an eternity.

> Promising to never let her go again, Mia now lives in the bed of the once little girl and reminisces of the crazy adventures that she led during her dog life. >

>

Raw adrenaline frequently substitutes for missing emotion and regard for the fallen. Yet perhaps the most corrosive aspect of FPS games is overlooked—the fundamental lack of concern for protagonists' lives that conventional game design fosters.
> 
> In his poem De rerum natura, Roman philosopher Lucretius argued that death is not a fundamental evil. Rather, the canonical end of life provides life with urgency and value. It imbues moments with viscerality—a minute with a sweetheart behind school bleachers, a child born, a momentous career victory—these instances gain meaning through their finite nature. The brevity and unpredictability of life makes every choice, from the life-changing to the mundane, meaningful. An immortal Commander Nick Reyes wouldn't understand that reality. My Nick Reyes does.

> A hot dog might be a sandwich, and cereal might be a soup, but is a ______ a ______?
> -Inspired by Arya Muralidharan, Class of 2021 (and dozens of others who, this year and in past years, have submitted the question “Is a hot dog a sandwich,” to which we reply, “maybe”)
> 
> Queuing can be unpleasant, but it’s something that I appreciate. It gives me an undisturbed opportunity to view my surroundings, a break from the fast tempo of life. 
> Is a queue a line? Maybe.
> Queuing is typically inevitable, encouraged by the social expectations of order and fairness, in other words, the “first come, first serve” or First In, First Out (FIFO) principle usually, yet I find it especially fascinating for the many implications it has. A “queue” and a “line,” while having equivalent definitions, may or may not be different when looking at it conceptually. 
> Strictly speaking, a physical queue could be graphically generalized into line segment(s) - not lines, which are infinitely long in one dimension - with some arrangement of points and segments between the start and end of a queuing system. As individuals take up space on a line, a queue could be equatable to a line: while a line is an arrangement of points or coordinates in some 2D or 3D space, a queue arranges individuals in the same space. Geometrically, a queue is a line. 
> When queues become crowds and those lines form shapes, the arrangement of individuals could be modeled like particles in a fluid, its outline based on its surrounding physical environment.
