**申请年份：** 2024/2025  
**学生数据：** SAT 1560

> As a child, I had a fascination with puzzles. I found I had an eye for solving them, confident that the pieces would form a complete picture if you just positioned them correctly. I later discovered through Grey's Anatomy that this logic I loved in puzzles translated into medicine; each case was a puzzle, every symptom a clue, and the doctors, professional puzzle-solvers, worked to fit everything into a final diagnosis. 
> 
> This sparked my interest in medicine and human anatomy, as I craved to understand how these pieces fit together into the complex organisms that we are. The more I learned, the more parts were involved, all connected to function seamlessly. For instance, as we studied in IB Biology, nervous signals trigger calcium release, enabling proteins to shift in muscle contraction: all involved in the deceptively simple act of lifting a finger or flexing an arm. Or during my internship at the Instituto Valenciano de Infertilidad, where I explored cell signaling in IVF, understanding how a single hormone like FSH can trigger a cascade of cellular responses that shape fundamental aspects of life, like fertility and reproduction.
> 
> Determined to further my passion, I've sought out opportunities to further immerse myself in the world of human physiology and medicine. During my internship at the Tilila Clinic in Morocco, I was finally able to experience my dream firsthand of shadowing doctors as they pieced together lab results, x-rays, and patient histories to solve real-life medical puzzles. Moreover, in the Medical Science Club I started at my school, I shared this passion with others as we took medical puzzle-solving into our own hands. Using our knowledge of organ systems and body processes, we worked to propose diagnoses and uncovered how different physiological systems are both impacted by but also contribute to disease. 
> 
> I have always heard that as a doctor, you never stop learning; there are endless puzzle pieces waiting to be found and connections yet to be made. Driven by my curiosity and the endless possibilities in medicine, I look forward to furthering this passion, and making meaningful and significant contributions.

**申请年份：** 2024/2025  
**学生数据：** SAT 1540

> I've always wished I could read minds. As a child, my people-pleasing tendencies crafted my sensitivity to others' thoughts and emotions. Over time, this compliance evolved into an interest in the motives behind people's behavior, guiding me towards psychology. 
> 
> Mentoring younger students inspired a curiosity about the behavioral changes that occur during adolescence. So, I centered my IB Extended Essay topic on the predictors to vaping initiation in UK adolescents. By referencing the Treynor's identity shift hypothesis and the social identity theory, I discussed how adolescents are especially vulnerable to social norms due to their fear of exclusion. Meanwhile, I reviewed how genes and neurodevelopment induce risk-taking behaviors. In short, substance-use behaviors are inseparable from a biological basis. 
> 
> Beyond the curriculum, I constructed a 22-page research proposal exploring how mentalization deficits in narcissistic abuse victims correlate with abnormal activities in the brain. For my methodology, I employed fMRI data with a triangulation of questionnaires, performance-based tasks, and interviews to comprehensively assess mentalization. Through this project, I learned how to select psychological measure for specific research aims. Furthermore, I familiarized myself with the neuroscientific dimension of human behaviors, which I can refer to in future research in psychology. 
> 
> Building on my interest in abnormal psychology, I completed the Stanford's online course on adolescent psychosis. I particularly appreciated how psychiatrists approach diagnosis and treatment with sensitivity, working to eradicate the cultural stigma and family misconceptions around mental illnesses. Growing up in China, where there is the discrimination against seeking therapy and a lack of mental health awareness, I witnessed firsthand how these barriers prevent younger generations from thriving in a high-pressure environment. 
> 
> To share my passion with the community, I co-founded a Criminology Club where we analyzed how criminal backgrounds motivated behaviors. From adverse childhood experiences to psychiatric abnormalities, we inspired debates that weighed how nature and nurture predicted crimes. 
> 
> In college, I am eager to draw inspiration from interacting with the diverse cohort while supporting peers who are navigating their voices through mentoring programs. For now, I've uncovered my superpower in 'mind-reading', or in academia terminology – mentalizing.

## Describe an example of your leadership experience  (350 words)

> "Describe an example of your leadership experience in which you have positively influenced others, helped resolve disputes or contributed to group efforts over time."

## 样本

### 01 ✅ 真实录取
**申请专业：** Biology  
**申请年份：** 2024/2025  
**学生数据：** SAT 1560

> Logging into the first Zoom call, I awkwardly introduced myself to 15 kids from the Khoutwa organization in Morocco whom I was tutoring in math. They stared back at me in silence as their supervisor translated my French into Darija (Moroccan-dialect). My confidence-- both in my ability to speak Arabic and to help them-- was nonexistent. Every pause stretched endlessly, the kids' eyes wandered, and the atmosphere felt heavy.
> 
> This uncomfortable silence continued for a few sessions, until one day, when a boy held up his notebook with the correct answer. Without thinking, I exclaimed "Meziane (good job)!". My broken Arabic drew surprised laughter and smiles from the kids, yet instead of embarrassment, I felt the atmosphere lighten, and the distance between us narrow, even just slightly.
> 
> As weeks went by, I got to know them better, and incorporated more snippets of Darija to hear them laugh. The silence of those early sessions soon gave way to excitement. We all became more comfortable, and I began to notice their progress. They used the strategies I taught them more often, and solved the problems I gave them with more confidence. By our final sessions, the dynamic had completely shifted. Now, when I gave them a problem to answer, the screen would light up with notebooks pushed eagerly toward the camera; every kid trying to get another meziane out of me. Even their supervisor, once translating my every word, sat back quietly, letting us carry the lesson on our own.
> 
> What began as a struggle to keep them focused turned into weekly sessions that became about more than just fractions or quadratics. I remember when one student, Amin, held up a test he'd aced: 9/10. His pride reminded me not only how far we'd come, but also how much I had learned with them. I realized that leadership, once a daunting idea to me, isn't about authority or perfection. It's about connection. As I watched their enthusiasm grow, I came to understand that helping others is a two-way street: one where you not only inspire others, but are also inspired in return.
