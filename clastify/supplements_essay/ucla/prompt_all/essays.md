> Logging into the first Zoom call, I awkwardly introduced myself to 15 kids from the Khoutwa organization in Morocco whom I was tutoring in math. They stared back at me in silence as their supervisor translated my French into Darija (Moroccan-dialect). My confidence-- both in my ability to speak Arabic and to help them-- was nonexistent. Every pause stretched endlessly, the kids' eyes wandered, and the atmosphere felt heavy.
> 
> This uncomfortable silence continued for a few sessions, until one day, when a boy held up his notebook with the correct answer. Without thinking, I exclaimed "Meziane (good job)!". >

By our final sessions, the dynamic had completely shifted. Now, when I gave them a problem to answer, the screen would light up with notebooks pushed eagerly toward the camera; every kid trying to get another meziane out of me. Even their supervisor, once translating my every word, sat back quietly, letting us carry the lesson on our own.
> 
> What began as a struggle to keep them focused turned into weekly sessions that became about more than just fractions or quadratics. I remember when one student, Amin, held up a test he'd aced: 9/10. His pride reminded me not only how far we'd come, but also how much I had learned with them. I realized that leadership, once a daunting idea to me, isn't about authority or perfection. It's about connection. As I watched their enthusiasm grow, I came to understand that helping others is a two-way street: one where you not only inspire others, but are also inspired in return.
