## Introduction / Background (Pages 1-5)

Research Question: How can we use Fourier Series to analyze and produce an Electrocardiogram signal?

Word Count: 2876 (notably below the 4000-word target)

The essay applies Fourier Series to model an ECG signal. An ECG is a periodic graphical representation of the heart's electrical activity. The author acknowledges from the outset that ECGs are not truly periodic ("no two ECG single pulses are identical"), but treats it as periodic for modelling purposes.

Structure:
1. Introduction (Fourier Series general form, ECG motivation)
2. Morphology of ECG (P-wave, PR interval, QRS Complex, ST interval, T-wave)
3. Decomposing the ECG signal
4. Forming a Mathematical Model (quadratic polynomials fitted to each wave, then Fourier coefficients computed)
5. Constructing Final Series
6. Conclusion

The mathematical model is built by:
- Fitting parabolas/Gaussians to each ECG wave using vertex form f(t) = a(t-h)² + k
- Computing Fourier coefficients a₀, aₙ, bₙ by integrating the piecewise model
- Summing up to 100 terms to reconstruct the ECG

## Conclusion / Bibliography

The essay notes that after graphing 10, 30, 75, and 100 Fourier terms, the reconstructed signal visually resembles the original ECG. Limitations acknowledged: assumed all ECG pulses are identical; mathematical model of ECG was hand-constructed (human error). The author found the result "very satisfying."

Bibliography: Wikipedia (Fourier Series), BMJ on ECG terminology, ECGlibrary.com, Khan Academy, Medscape, and Gaussian function (Wikipedia). Only 8 references — relatively thin.

Research Question: To what extent Shpilkin's method of election analysis is applicable to the analysis of electoral results to detect fraud?

Word Count: 3913 words

The essay analyzes Shpilkin's method — a statistical technique developed by Russian physicist/mathematician Sergei Shpilkin to detect electoral fraud by examining distributions of voter turnout and vote shares at polling stations. Applied to the 2024 Russian presidential election.

Structure:
1. Introduction
2. Shpilkin's method description (graphical interpretation; mathematical background; advantages/disadvantages)
3. Model applicability to Russian 2024 elections
4. Evaluation
5. Conclusion / Bibliography / Appendices

The Shpilkin method assumes that in fair elections, voter turnout and vote distributions across polling stations follow a bivariate normal (Gaussian) distribution — most stations cluster around average turnout, with fewer showing extreme values. Anomalies (spikes at round-number turnout percentages, excess votes concentrated at high turnout) indicate possible fraud. The essay applies this to Russian 2024 data, comparing to known-fair elections (2018 Czech, 2017 French presidential elections) as benchmarks.

Personal motivation: Candidate lived in Russia for six years and witnessed the 2018 presidential election firsthand.

The essay examines the relationship between the Prime Counting Function π(x) and the Riemann Zeta Function ζ(s), contextualizing their similarities within the Riemann Hypothesis. The author was initially drawn to the functions' apparent resemblance, then discovered the Riemann Hypothesis as the appropriate framework.

Structure:
1. Introduction
2. Investigation and planning
3. Body:
   - Variables and constants (π(x), ln x, ζ(s), Li(x), Ψ(x) Riemann-Siegel formula)

Key finding: Despite the essay's research question asking about "similarities," the conclusion states that "there are no significant similarities between the functions...except for their shared purpose in prime number distribution." The connection lies in the zeta zeros: as more zeta zeros are included in the explicit formula for π(x), the Riemann Zeta Function approximates the Prime Counting Function more closely.

## Conclusion / Evaluation

Conclusion: The zeta zeros are intimately related to the distribution of prime numbers. Comparing the two functions allows prediction of prime placement via zeta zeros. The Riemann Hypothesis remains an open problem concerning the zeros of ζ(s).

Evaluation — Strengths: all graphs made programmatically; basic function properties included; critical thinking in conclusion; coherent structure.
Evaluation — Weaknesses: graphs need more variables; topic already broadly researched with high entry threshold; word count notably below 4000.
