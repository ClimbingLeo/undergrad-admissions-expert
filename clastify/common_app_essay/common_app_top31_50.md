## Essay — Prompt #4

> Reflect on something that someone has done for you that has made you happy or thankful in a surprising way. How has this gratitude affected or motivated you?

Overtime, I witnessed the story of my mother's hands, canvas of the Mekong River map, etched with the scars of countless knife cuts. Layered upon these scars were rugged patches of skin, evidence of hours spent toiling over chores. Her hands grew coarser and drier from the repetitive tasks of hand-washing clothes and dishes, activities she considered to be a mark of "authentic labor." Some days, her nails took on shades of yellow on curcumin; other days, they turned to brown from gardening, yet to her, the colors bore no significance. My father's hands mirrored hers in many ways. They were weathered, broad, and dry. But it didn't come from the same household chemicals my mother used. Instead, it was due to his daily encounters with the maintenance diesel that is required to keep the CNC laser machines running. Over time, his hands developed dry patches adorned with the occasional tiny fluid pocket. Mine, on the other hand, were different. I'm exempted from all kinds of chores. Not because I resisted, but because my parents were certain that these hands of mine have other pursuits. They are made for academic and artistic pursuits. And to my parent's expectations, even after years of playing the piano and painting, and extensive hours of practicing and painting, my hands maintained their softness and fairness but most importantly, weren't full of blisters. In the midst of this artistic journey, I collected an array of esteemed rewards. But deep down, I understood that they were only possible through the unwavering support of my parents. I was a kid with little life experience beyond the predetermined path I was expected to follow. I was complacent with my life pursuits. The path was determined, guided by the strokes of my own hands and the echoes of countless piano keys played at my parents' behest. I was burdened by an ever-deepening gratitude for my parents' sacrifices and guilt for the potential version of myself that remained untapped. My hands, once accustomed to the gentle touch of piano keys and paintbrushes, were now poised to grasp new challenges and set my true potential free. From a young age, I had a knack for creating value, but it was the art of capitalizing on those values that remained a mystery to me. Making money was a desire, not from just any endeavor, but from hard-core labor, and I was determined to do it with my own hands. This drive led me to establish The Young Entrepreneurs, with the primary goal of not only earning but also helping school kids discover their business acumen, using both their minds and hands. After conducting rounds of market research, it became clear that food and drinks were in high demand within the school. So, we opened a snack stall during every break, to paint smiles on the student's faces during the scorching heat of Saigon. In the midst of those scorching, sweltering days, my hands and my team member's hands became the very life force of the break time sales. They toiled tirelessly under the relentless sun. The shouts of "Change, change, change!" from the students echoed as we handed out food and drinks, and my fingers, weathered and worn from ceaselessly peeling away countless layers of packaging, served as a testament to my unwavering labor.

After months of sales, we had enough money to make an impact. I decided to donate all our profits to the Brighter Path Scholarship Program. It felt right. It felt right to pass on the fruits of our labor to our less fortunate peers, knowing that Ha, a 19-year-old bright woman from the program that we had the chance to meet, would be able to pursue her dream of higher education. And many others like her, would be able to embark on their journey of self-development. It was then that I learned the power of these hands that my parents have shown me all along.

## Essay — Prompt #5

> Discuss an accomplishment, event, or realization that sparked a period of personal growth and a new understanding of yourself or others.

The dark, spacious ballroom fills with warm light emitted by large crystal chandeliers. A girl in an old ripped dress exchanges elegant bows with translucent figures entering the hall. As the ball begins, the guests circle Anastasia, dancing gracefully to her dreamy song. With the power of my namesake's imagination, her dress turns into a luminous yellow gown, with its bright sparkles reflecting in my teary eyes. It was a frosty winter evening in my Siberian hometown. My mother is braiding my hair as we sit on the couch in front of the TV. The "Best of 20th Century Fox" CD's spinning noise is interrupted by my sniffing. "What happened?" my mom asks. "Nothing. This ball was just very beautiful." "You are so sensitive," she replies with a hearty smile. I was seven when I heard this phrase for the first time. While my mom did not mean it as an insult, it later became one I was constantly told. My elementary school teacher scolded me for crying over bad grades. My 7th-grade classmates called me a baby for being upset about a dead bird on the windowsill. Due to constantly being told I had to "grow up" and "hold myself together", I was convinced that sensitivity was my biggest weakness, and that it made me irrational and immature. Constantly pretending to be thick-skinned, and hiding emotions felt necessary to earn respect and exhausted me. Years have gone by. Anastasia from the cartoon became a Disney princess, and Anastasiia, who is writing this essay, is now nineteen. She moved away from her parents, and her hair is too short to be braided. Nonetheless, there she is, once again, tearing up from the enchanting song and animation of the “Once Upon a December” royal ball scene of the movie "Anastasia". I paused the movie and reflected. Yes, quite often, a tear runs down my cheek before I can process why. Yes, sometimes I laugh and love and stress and rage all in the same hour. Yes, sometimes it feels like an emotion, good or bad, is so intense that it fills up the entirety of me and glows through my paper-thin skin for everyone to see. And you know what? Regardless of what society tried to convince me of, sensitivity is not my weakness—it is actually my biggest strength.

I believe that my sensitive nature is the reason I started making art at a young age: I saw art making as a way of feeling someone else's and expressing my own emotions in the most tangible and aesthetically pleasing ways. As I grew older, my excitement about animated movies became accompanied by a keen interest in cinema, video games, art history, and design. Attentively observing the diverse aesthetics of various art media, I subconsciously developed an artistic taste and a deep love for art. I gradually acquired a keen eye for detail, and I am able to pick up on nuances that others might miss, like particular colour combinations and their impacts on the audience. As a result of gaining a high visual capacity, I am able to learn effectively from creators that I admire, which is a crucial skill for a professional artist that strives to continuously learn and advance. To this day, I see the world through high-saturation glasses and perceive my surroundings as vibrant and dramatic. Hence, my sensitivity nourishes my creativity and pushes my art skills, helping me continuously discover inspiration in ordinary things. My empathetic nature not only makes me a kind friend, but also an endlessly inspired leader of ambitious artistic projects that bring positive change to surrounding communities. Long story short, I will definitely cry once I receive the college decision—whether from extreme joy or deep sadness. But this is who I am, and I am proud of it: I strive to embrace my sensitive nature and the strengths it brings.

It's an Indian tradition akin to bowing in respect at the temple: your parents choose what you study. My father, a Chartered Accountant by profession, insisted on particular courses motivated by my academic skills and practicality; if you have an aptitude for STEM, it has to be engineering or medicine. He emphasized the value of physics and computer science, but I was more passionate about research and struggled to balance his counsel with my goals. My dream of exploring biological psychology was perceived as a whim or rebellion. It was a difficult road which eventually set the tone to how I would navigate through the IBDP and beyond. My intense urge to delve into the labyrinthine web of life was sparked by my grandfather's agonizing struggle with Parkinson's disease and its effect on my family. Every month, during the followup meeting with the doctor, I would pepper him with questions. At home, my family had taken an ostrich mentality to cope. My constant barrage of information about symptoms and palliative care caused many heated arguments. Even during my childhood, I have seen my parents fight frequently over things that seemed unimportant to me, their protracted arguments impacting my mental and emotional health. As a pre-teen I struggled with the changes happening with me and around me. I became conflict-averse; simple decisions like standing up to a friend who was ill-treating me or haggling with the auto-rickshaw driver for a ride share, stressed me out. Perturbed, I withdrew from my friends, grappling with sleepless nights. When I worked on my Extended Essay on "To what extent does parental marital discord increase the prevalence of anxiety in adolescents?" I realized the many unaddressed scars I was carrying. My research threw light not just on the wider consequences for teenagers in similar situations but also sharpened my inherent motivation to uncover what I can do to reduce my anxiety and bolster my mental health. I understood that following my own interests and talents is the key to finding true fulfillment. Whether I'm examining the delicate dance of molecular interactions and ecosystems or dissecting the intricate symphony of human intellect, emotion and social dynamics, I find that I'm driven to unraveling the mystifying threads that bind life together. My desire to grasp the world's complexities via the lenses of science and psychology is guided by these highly personal experiences, which have shed light in the

direction I want to go. It is through them that I know my place, and the impact I can create. It took a while to find my feet, but I have resolved to take a proactive stance. I have steadily grown in academic scores by working on my weak areas. I pursued an online course offered by Harvard University to grow my understanding of biological sciences. The Fundamentals of Neuroscience, taught by Professor David Cox, cemented my fascination with the brain's astounding complexity and the social relationships it facilitates. I particularly enjoyed using lessons such as brain puzzles and reaction timers from the DIY labs curriculum to help my family understand the vast realm of answers that biology holds. Today, I handle issues in my daily life with tolerance, empathy, and a willingness to establish common ground because of the experiences I have navigated. I no longer go into fight or flight mode when my parents disagree with me or my friends have a falling out. Every time I'm asked to toe someone else's line, instead of arguing or retaliating with hurtful words, I choose to voice my opinions calmly and be brave enough to keep my own counsel. I am learning to establish a common ground with myself, because I am that neuron of hope I am not willing to shut out.

## Essay — Prompt #1

> Some students have a background, identity, interest, or talent that is so meaningful they believe their application would be incomplete without it. If this sounds like you, then please share your story.

My father has a perfect encyclopedic knowledge of everything, at least in his mind. He would never confess to being at fault and never treated me like a son; instead, I was his intellectual inferior. Whenever he's mentioned, I always get a sense of wistfulness because I know it's half my fault that we're so disjointed, but our cuts were never equal; critiques, insults, and vitriol were normalized in our dysfunctional dynamic. Unfortunately, those remarks maimed me, and the fear of judgment became the motif of my life. Needless to say, I was a selective introvert. I forewent opportunities to foster relationships with others because I was socially paranoid. I even envied how seemingly self-assured everybody was with their social cliques and how my insecurities encompassed everything about others. I was enigmatic, permanently closed off. My best friend was my MacBook Air, the only person I could rely on throughout my turmoil. Enter October 1, 2017, the day I installed the game of my generation: Roblox. There, I found solace. It was my form of escape and instrument of happiness. When I'm feeling overwhelmed or plain tired, I return to my virtual friends that I would never have the courage to talk to in person. My mom always yelled at me for spending too much time on the screen. "Sai lou," she shouted in Cantonese, "Stop gaming!" But nothing could stop me; it was a scary obsession. When the pandemic hit, my addiction to Roblox grew more potent. Although I tried deluding myself into being content with isolation, I longed more and more to search for my own 'social clique,' one with which I could share my quirks. Then came an idea: in the fall of 2020, I decided to dabble in Roblox YouTube content creation. I transformed my room into a makeshift studio, where I focused all my time learning the intricacies of IMovie and researched the geekier aspects of YouTube: CTR, SEO, and audience retention. Soon after, the buzz surrounding my channel grew; each video I published became a hit. Although I avoided the spotlight in school, I welcomed it online. I cultivated a devoted fanbase that shares the same passions for gaming and even had people mimicking me on TikTok. I essentially had a full-time job at 14, while attending school via Zoom. It became my life, yet it didn't matter because it was my passion and my only escape from self-suppression. The adrenaline and sense of accomplishment I received from hitting my first milestones - 100,000 subscribers, 1,000,000 views, 10,000,000 impressions - just kept snowballing. It felt like something out of a Disney Movie, like I had used one of my three wishes.

Over the following months, this marked my transition from being insecure to self-assured. My channel's success translated into real life, where I began feeling comfortable willingly talking to others in person (without being forced into a group project). However, life is more convoluted. My priorities began to become blurred as I sought to focus more of my attention on building meaningful, non-pixelated connections rather than online ones, something I've been longing for my entire life. It took me a while to realize the value of real-life connections, but allocating all my time to creating videos prohibited me from doing so. I'd been able to live my online aspirations, but blindsided by the opportunities I missed out to appreciate human presence. So I decided to quit. I know there's a huge opportunity cost in doing so, but I would rather let YouTube be an aspect of my life, not my whole life. After this experience, I adopted a new mindset: balancing being ambitious yet affable, pragmatic yet playful, and hardworking while staying humble. Now, I feel comfortable sharing my truth while surrounded by others who help keep me grounded. Looking forward, I'm thrilled to meet new people that embrace and empower me; but fundamentally, I'm looking forward to proving myself and testing my new capabilities.

Taal and Laya (Beat and Rhythm) “Alright, let's go again. No sleep till it's perfect.” A collective groan filled the room when our music Guru asked us to practise the beats for the tenth time in the past two hours. We were exhausted from continuous practice and little sleep; every member of our dance group was itching to go back to bed. While we all wanted the exams to end as soon as possible so that we could go back to the comfort of our daily lives, none of us wanted our efforts in the past few years to go to waste. In this long journey, we had the opportunity to know and understand each other quite well. Memories of our six-hour long weekend classes where we sat together reciting and perfecting entire songs, came flooding back. Throughout the journey, I focused on gaining knowledge of the art and its techniques. I accepted everything including the admonishments of our Guru as a part of the learning process. However, standing in the exam hall and looking at my friends, I realized that most of my learning was not about the practice of the art, but about becoming humble and sensitive towards others. The journey of becoming a certified dancer presented many unexpected moments and taught me how to make the best out of any given condition. Watching our Guru have a breakdown a day before the practical and still keep a brave face for the students, taught us that we were all in the same boat. Our support for each other became our strength. We shared laughter, ate at the same table and shared even our smallest happiness with each other to reduce our stress. Even when we had to share one room and a single bathroom among the six of us, I didn't feel overwhelmed. Instead, we learned how to adjust and accommodate each other's needs. No limitations, whether it was sleeping on a mattress on the cold floor without any pillows, or lack of hot water for bathing in winters, could deter or break our spirits.

My trip to the city of Hyderabad became a lifelong lesson where I learned never to take the good things in life for granted, and always stand up for others. As we completed the exams and headed back home, I realised that I had learned more about life and the world of art than ever before. For one, I will never be taking my bed for granted ever again.
